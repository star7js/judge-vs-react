\title{\textbf{Judge vs.\ React:\\ dissociating perception from timing in VLM reflex control}}
\author{Josh Simnitt\\ \small\texttt{jsimnitt@gmail.com}}
\date{2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A vision-language-model (VLM) agent that must time a single action in a
first-person scene can fail two ways that existing game benchmarks cannot pull
apart: it can misjudge the scene (perception), or judge correctly but act too
late (timing). We build a minimal, deterministic first-person pit-jump task that
reduces an episode to one decision, labels each decision in- or out-of-window
from the sim's own dynamics, and injects a control delay $D$ so that lateness
varies independently of judgment. In the dimensionless coordinate $r = vD/W$
(distance travelled during the delay over window width), a policy that fires at
the window's leading edge clears iff $r < 1$ by construction; oracle sweeps on
the pit ($\rstar = 1.04$, the edge bracketed within one tick of delay at every
pit width) and on a second rotational-aiming task ($\rstar = 0.98$) confirm that the
apparatus reproduces this, and serve as calibration rather than as an empirical
law. The main result is a \textbf{real model swept through that boundary}: a
closed-loop Sonnet~4.6 agent, judging every frame itself, clears \textbf{32/32}
in-window decisions below $r \approx 1$ and \textbf{0/26} above it
($\rstar = 1.05$), with nothing varied but the injected delay --- the same
correct decision succeeds paused and fails late. Past the boundary its
\emph{only} surviving episodes are ones that misjudged early, their perception
error cancelled by the delay: the two failure axes compensate, which an
aggregate score cannot see. On the perception axis, blind single-frame judgment
at zero delay scales with model capability (Opus 90\%, Sonnet 80\%) and
collapses to chance under fog. Measuring five real vision models' decision
latency against the task's 257\,ms window, \textbf{all land past the boundary}
--- hosted frontier models at $r \approx 4$--$6$, local open VLMs at
$r \approx 31$--$47$ --- so none can act inside the window in real time, yet the
same decisions succeed when the world is paused. Pausable/turn-based deployment
converts a slow-but-accurate VLM from failing to viable, and ``VLMs are too slow
to play games'' is partly an artifact of the time regime they are benchmarked
in.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When a vision-language model plays a real-time game and walks into a pit, the
post-mortem is ambiguous. Did it fail to \emph{see} that a jump was needed
(perception), or did it see correctly and \emph{act too late} (timing)?
Aggregate game benchmarks --- score, win rate, levels cleared --- fold these two
failures into one number, so ``the VLM is bad at this game'' can mean either,
and usually means some unknown mix. The two have opposite fixes: a perception
failure wants a better model; a timing failure wants a slower clock. Conflating
them is why ``VLMs are too slow to play games'' is repeated as though it were a
single fact.

Our move is isolation. We reduce an episode to \emph{one} correct decision --- a
first-person pit the player auto-walks toward, where the only choice is
\emph{when} to jump --- so that on any given run exactly one of the two failures
can occur, and we can say which. The apparatus is deterministic and headless,
and it injects a control delay $D$ (a decision made at tick $t$ is applied at
$t{+}D$), which lets us vary \emph{lateness} completely independently of
\emph{judgment quality}: a perfect-judgment oracle still fails if its action
lands too late.

The headline result is \textbf{S5}: a closed-loop Sonnet~4.6 agent, judging
every frame itself, is swept through the boundary by injected delay and clears
32/32 in-window decisions below $r \approx 1$ and 0/26 above it, and its only
clears past the boundary are \emph{delay-rescued misjudgments}. Around it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Calibration (S1, S2, S4).} For a policy that fires at the
    window's leading edge, clearing iff $r < 1$ is arithmetic, not a discovery.
    Oracle sweeps on the pit (S1; $\rstar = 1.04$ on a 1-tick delay grid that
    brackets the edge at every width) and on a rotational aiming task (S4;
    $\rstar = 0.98$) check that the sim's dynamics, the in-window label, and
    $r$ reproduce it without leaking tolerance, and the oracle's same-decision
    flip (S2) previews S5 on a perfect judge.
  \item \textbf{Perception axis.} Blind single-frame judgment at zero delay
    scales with model capability and collapses to chance under fog --- only in
    the models that read depth.
  \item \textbf{Latency axis.} Five real vision models' decision latencies
    against the task's 257\,ms window: all past the boundary, hosted frontier
    models at $r \approx 4$--$6$ and local open VLMs at $r \approx 31$--$47$.
  \item \textbf{Control (S3).} Action-repeat adds polling delay and shifts the
    transition to lower explicit $r$; it is a cost lever, not a latency fix.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

Five works are closest; we differentiate from each.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OmniGameArena} (Lin et al., 2026, arXiv:2606.09826) --- has
    paused (PDQ) vs.\ latency-controlled real-time (LCRT) clock modes, but only
    as \textbf{aggregate whole-game} comparisons; no per-decision in-window
    label, no single-decision flip. We add both, and the $r$ coordinate that
    makes latency measurements comparable across tasks.
  \item \textbf{VideoGameBench} (Zhang et al., 2025, arXiv:2505.18134) ---
    ``Lite'' (paused) vs.\ real-time; names the stale-action mechanism but
    leaves perception confounded (``it's not just latency''). We resolve exactly
    that confound by isolating one decision.
  \item \textbf{``Win Fast or Lose Slow''} (Kang et al., NeurIPS 2025,
    arXiv:2505.19481) --- owns the latency--quality tradeoff, but its latency
    lever is quantization/model-size, which \emph{also} degrades quality, and
    its $\approx$200\,ms point is an environment action-rate cap, not a
    task-window threshold. Our sweep varies latency \textbf{independently} of
    the model --- the apparatus injects control delay $D$ with the policy
    fixed.
  \item \textbf{BLINK} (Fu et al., ECCV 2024, arXiv:2404.12390) --- canonical
    ``MLLMs fail at depth,'' but static MCQ. Ours is interactive, parametric,
    oracle-normalized.
  \item \textbf{Ramstedt \& Pal, ``Real-Time RL''} (NeurIPS 2019,
    arXiv:1911.04448) [+ ``At Human Speed,'' arXiv:1810.07286] --- the RL parent
    of the real-time/delay result (monotone degradation for learned policies).
    We express the same delay cost as $r$ for \emph{pausable VLM agents}, show
    a real VLM crossing it, and show turn-based deployment stepping back over
    it.
\end{itemize}

We explicitly do \textbf{not} claim to be first to study the latency--quality
tradeoff (Win Fast) nor first to isolate timing from competence in the aggregate
(Real-Time Deadlines, arXiv:2601.13206; OmniGameArena). Our claim is the
single-decision dissociation on a real model, the compensating-axes observation
past the boundary, and the quality-controlled $r$ coordinate that makes the
latency measurements interpretable.

\section{Task and apparatus}

A first-person raycaster. The player auto-walks toward a pit; the only decision
is \emph{when} to jump. Knobs: pit width (sets the action-window $W$),
fog/visibility (perception), approach speed $v$, seed. The sim is deterministic
and headless; control delay $D$ (ticks) is \textbf{injected} --- a decision made
at tick $t$ is applied at $t{+}D$ --- so $D$ is an independent variable
decoupled from wall-clock. Real per-model latency $L$ is measured separately
(timed calls) and mapped to the $r$ axis via $L/\text{tick}$. An oracle policy
(true pit distance) is the perfect-judgment ceiling.

\paragraph{Ground-truth label.} \code{in\_window} $=$ whether a delay-0 takeoff
at the decision position clears, computed from the sim's \emph{own} dynamics
(not an analytic approximation), so \code{in\_window} exactly predicts
\code{cleared} at $D{=}0$. The action window is $W = w_{hi} - w_{lo}$ with
$w_{lo}, w_{hi}$ derived by simulating takeoffs; for pit widths beyond the jump
reach the window is empty ($W \le 0$, unclearable) and those configs are
excluded from fits (reported, not silently dropped).
\[
  r = \frac{v \cdot D}{W}\ \text{(oracle sweep)}
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  r = \frac{v \cdot (L/\text{tick})}{W}\ \text{(real-model placement).}
\]

\paragraph{What $r$ does and does not tell you.} A policy that commits at the
window's leading edge $w_{lo}$ has its delayed takeoff land at $w_{lo} + vD$,
which clears iff $vD < W$, i.e.\ iff $r < 1$. The location of the boundary is
therefore fixed by where the policy fires, not discovered by sweeping; a policy
that fired at the window's center would place it at $r = 0.5$. $r$ is the
coordinate in which that statement is exact and task-independent, which is what
makes a measured latency $L$ interpretable against any window. The oracle
sweeps in \S4.1--4.3 are calibration: they check that the dynamics, the label,
and $r$ reproduce the boundary with no leaked tolerance. The question with
empirical content --- where a \emph{real} judge's commit point falls, and
whether its outcome flips at that boundary with nothing else varied --- is
\S4.4.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Calibration: the oracle boundary (S1)}

The oracle fires at the first in-window position, so a delayed takeoff lands at
$w_{lo} + vD$ and clears iff $r < 1$; the sweep's job is to confirm that the
apparatus reproduces this. It does. The default grid (4 pit widths $\times$ 7
delays, $D \in \{0, 4, \dots, 24\}$) has 28 unique configurations. The 1680
committed rows replicate each $60\times$ across fog and seed, which the oracle
ignores (every replicate is identical), and the 7 configurations at pit width 4
have an empty window ($W \le 0$, unclearable) and are excluded. On the
remaining 21 configurations the pooled logistic fit gives $\rstar = 1.17$, but
that number is a grid artifact: the last clear is at $r = 1.04$ and the first
failure at $r = 1.30$, both at pit width 2, and $1.17$ is the logistic midpoint
of a 4-tick gap. The other widths do not straddle the edge within this grid ---
width 1 ($W = 2.08$) never exceeds $r = 0.81$ and always clears, and width 3
($W = 0.08$) is already at $r = 3.5$ at the first nonzero delay and always
fails.

A 1-tick delay grid ($D \in \{0, \dots, 40\}$, widths 1--3, one seed, fog 0;
123 configurations) removes the artifact. Each width brackets the edge within a
single tick of delay: width 1 between $r = 1.01$ ($D = 30$) and $1.04$
($D = 31$), width 2 between $1.04$ ($D = 16$) and $1.10$ ($D = 17$), and width 3
between $0.89$ ($D = 1$) and $1.77$ ($D = 2$), its window being only 1.1 ticks
wide. Every width is a clean step (every delay below its edge clears, every
delay at or above it fails), and the pooled logistic fit gives
$\rstar = 1.04$. This is the calibration number used below. The clears just
past $r = 1$ are physical: a jump that fires on the same tick can take off from
just past the near edge, extending tolerance a fraction of a tick beyond the
nominal window. The transition is a step because the oracle is deterministic.

\subsection{Same-decision flip on the oracle (S2)}

Restricting to oracle-correct decisions (\code{in\_window} $= 1$; the oracle's
commit position does not depend on $D$), the \textbf{identical} decision clears
3/3 unique configurations at $D = 0$ and 0/8 once $r \ge 1.30$. The one
in-window decision past $r = 1$ that clears is the $r = 1.04$ same-tick case
above. Only execution timing changed. We do not summarize this as a pooled
probability: six of the 9 configurations with $r > 1$ are at pit width 3, where
$r$ runs from $3.5$ to $21$, so a rate over them would describe the delay grid
rather than the task. The flip on a real judge, with decisions on both sides of
the edge, is S5.

\subsection{The same calibration on a second task (S4)}

Is the apparatus's behaviour specific to jump-timing? We built a second task in
a different modality: a \textbf{sweeping-aim} task. The player rotates in place
at constant angular speed $\omega$; a target subtends an angular window
$[\theta - h, \theta + h]$ (width $2h$); the single decision is \emph{fire}, and
a control delay $D$ rotates the aim $\omega D$ further before the shot lands ---
overshoot if it exceeds the window. By construction the dimensionless cost is
$r = \omega D / (2h)$, the exact angular analog of the pit's $vD/W$. The aim
episode is pure kinematics and emits the same schema, so the \emph{identical}
fit machinery applies.

Because the aim oracle also fires at the leading edge, a hit iff $r < 1$ is
again arithmetic; the test is whether an independent implementation of the
kinematics and the label reproduces it. It does: across 205 unique
configurations at five target widths (4.6\textdegree--13.8\textdegree; one
seed, the aim oracle being deterministic) the pooled logistic fit gives
$\rstar = 0.98$, and at every width the last hit lies at $r = 0.87$--$0.96$ and
the first miss at exactly $r = 1.00$. The offset from the pit's $1.04$ is the
oracles' firing phase: the pit's takeoff can extend just \emph{past} the near
edge (a hair above 1), while the aim fires just \emph{inside} the near edge,
forfeiting a sub-tick of tolerance (a hair below 1). Two tasks agreeing on
$r \approx 1$ shows that $r$ is the right coordinate for both and that neither
implementation leaks tolerance. It is not evidence of an empirical law: a
policy that fired at the window's center would place its boundary at $r = 0.5$
in the same apparatus. What the next section asks is where a real judge's
commit point actually falls.

\subsection{A real model through the boundary (S5)}

The oracle sections calibrate the boundary; here a real model is swept
\emph{through} it with its own judgment in the loop. Because the apparatus
decouples sim time from wall-clock, the model's (slow, variable) API latency is
free, and $r$ is engineered exactly as in S1: Sonnet~4.6 judges \emph{every
grounded frame} of a closed-loop blocking episode (pit width 2, $v = 0.07$,
fog 0, seeds 1--6), its committed jump is applied $D$ ticks later, and sweeping
$D \in \{0, \dots, 31\}$ sweeps $r = vD/W$ from $0$ to $2$, densely around
$r = 1$ (72 episodes; 6{,}612 vision calls).

\paragraph{Judge protocol.} The episode prompt used for the latency battery
(\S4.6) turns out never to elicit JUMP near the window from \emph{any} hosted
model: it describes the pit as ``the black band across the floor ahead,'' which
stops matching the close-range view --- inside the window the pit is a black
region filling the bottom of the frame, and the visible checkered floor is the
pit's \emph{far} side. The S5 judge instead answers a literal perceptual
question (``what touches the bottom border of the image: tiles or black?'') at
high reasoning effort, with a commit rule of two consecutive JUMP answers. The
commit rule matters: in prompt-development batteries we measured isolated false
JUMPs at the 1--2\% per-call level far from the pit, and any such rate fires
almost surely somewhere over the $\sim$90 consulted approach frames --- a
first-passage failure mode that single-frame accuracy batteries cannot see. We
validated the final judge on all 108 approach frames $\times$ 3 samples: zero
far-field JUMPs, unanimous JUMP inside the window, and isolated misfires only in
the last half-tile before the window ($x = 8.10$--$8.24$) --- the source of the
9/72 early commits below. This judge was developed against, and validated on,
the same renderer, pit width, and fog level the sweep uses; it is a judge tuned
to this scene, and we make no claim that it transfers. The bottom-edge cue is
nearly ideal in this renderer: the pit's black region first touches the frame's
bottom edge at $x = 9.01$, one tick after the window opens ($w_{lo} = 8.92$).

\paragraph{The boundary holds for a real judge.} Episodes whose decision lands
in the window clear \textbf{32/32 through $r = 0.97$}, 4/5 at $r = 1.04$, and
\textbf{0/26 from $r = 1.10$ on}; the same logistic fit as S1 gives
$\rstar = 1.05$, coinciding with the oracle's width-2 bracket $[1.04, 1.10]$
on the 1-tick grid: the real judge's edge sits where the perfect judge's does,
same-tick tolerance included. The transition is
nearly as sharp as the oracle's, for an unplanned reason: the model's commit
position is almost deterministic --- 51/72 episodes commit at $x = 8.94$ and 12
at $x = 9.01$, straddling the cue's pixel-level onset (the model begins
answering ``black'' one tick before the black region literally reaches the
bottom edge at $x = 9.01$). The anticipated noisy psychometric curve compresses
to a near-step; this judge's decision-position variance is sub-tick, so the
model behaves as a \emph{slightly-early oracle} rather than a noisy one. Given
that commit point, the \emph{location} of $\rstar$ follows from the kinematics
exactly as in S1. What S5 establishes that the oracle cannot is the
dissociation itself on a real judge: the same model, making the same
frame-by-frame decisions, clears every in-window decision below the boundary
and none above it, with nothing varied but $D$.

\paragraph{Delay-rescued misjudgments.} 9/72 episodes commit early
($x = 8.10$--$8.24$, out of window --- a perception error; these are excluded
from the timing fit above). At $D \le 8$ every such episode fails, landing
short. In the band $D = 15$--$24$ ($r = 0.97$--$1.56$), \emph{every} early episode
\textbf{clears} (5/5): the delay pushes the too-early takeoff into the window.
Past $r \approx 1.1$ the \emph{only} surviving episodes are the misjudged
ones; by $r = 2.01$ the delay overshoots even the early takeoff and the
rescue band closes. The two failure axes do not merely
dissociate --- on opposite sides of the boundary they \emph{compensate}, which
is only visible because the apparatus labels each decision in- or
out-of-window.

\subsection{Perception axis}

A capable vision model \emph{can} judge the jump from a single frame, and fog
collapses it. We rendered standing decision frames across approach positions at
two visibility levels and had \textbf{four blind vision models} --- three Claude
models (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet, Opus) and one local open VLM (\code{qwen2.5-vl:7b}
via Ollama) --- each shown only the image (no coordinates, window, or labels;
identical seed-42 shuffled frame set) call JUMP or WAIT on each; we score each
call against the ground-truth window. Judgment accuracy at zero delay ($n = 10$
frames per model per condition):

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Blind single-frame judgment accuracy at zero delay
  ($n = 10$ frames per model per condition; exact counts shown).}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Model & Clear (fog=0) & Foggy (fog=1) & Fog effect \\
    \midrule
    Opus                    & \textbf{90\%} (9/10) & 50\% (5/10, chance) & $\mathbf{-40}$ pts \\
    Sonnet                  & 80\% (8/10)          & 50\% (5/10, chance) & $\mathbf{-30}$ pts \\
    Qwen2.5-VL 7B (local)   & 60\% (6/10)          & 60\% (6/10)         & 0 \\
    Haiku 4.5               & 40\% (4/10)          & 40\% (4/10)         & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two things fall out. \textbf{(1) With a clear depth cue, judgment scales with
model capability} --- Opus 90\%, Sonnet 80\%, Qwen 60\%, Haiku 40\%. \textbf{(2)
The fog collapse appears only in the models that actually read depth.} Opus and
Sonnet drop to chance under fog, and each \textbf{missed every in-window frame}
(0/5): they reported no distinguishable pit band even up close, never committed,
and would fall. The two weak judges show \textbf{no fog effect at all} --- but
for opposite-looking reasons that are really the same reason: Haiku sits at
floor (it fell back on a frame-order heuristic the shuffle nullifies), and Qwen
is strongly \emph{JUMP-biased} (it answered JUMP on 18/20 frames, clearing all
in-window frames but also most out-of-window ones). Neither is using the depth
cue in the first place, so neither has any perception to lose when fog removes
it. The fog collapse is a \textbf{signature of genuine depth reading}, present
exactly where clear-condition accuracy is high. At $n = 10$ frames per cell the
per-model contrasts are directional rather than individually significant
(Fisher exact, clear vs.\ fog: Opus $p = 0.14$, Sonnet $p = 0.35$; pooled
Opus$+$Sonnet $17/20$ vs.\ $10/20$, $p = 0.04$) --- it is the coherence of the
pattern across four models, not any single cell, that carries the claim.

This is the perception half of the dissociation: a model that judges well with a
clear depth cue drops to chance when fog obscures the pit's distance ---
\textbf{at zero delay}, i.e.\ independent of the timing axis. Together,
perception and timing are separable failure axes on one task. (Caveat: the
Claude judges run via a subagent path, not a controlled API benchmark; the local
VLM is a real keyless run. Judges are blinded to coordinates and labels but not
to the task.)

\subsection{Real models' latency against the window}

Because the decision cost is exactly $r = L / T_{\text{window}}$, where the
window is open for $T_{\text{window}} = (W/v)\cdot\text{tick} = 257\,\text{ms}$
at pit width 2 ($W = 1.078$, $v = 0.07$, tick $= 1/60\,$s), the $\rstar$
boundary sets a \textbf{maximum tolerable decision latency of
$\rstar\cdot T_{\text{window}} \approx 270\,$ms}. We timed five vision models
on the identical 20-frame decision battery, in each model's \emph{fastest}
single-call configuration ($n = 20$ each):

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Measured per-model decision latency (median of $n = 20$ single-frame
  calls, fastest configuration) against the 257\,ms window at pit width 2.}
  \begin{tabular}{lrr}
    \toprule
    Model & Median $L$ & $r = L / \text{window}$ \\
    \midrule
    Haiku 4.5 (hosted API)        & 0.95\,s  & $\approx 4$ \\
    Sonnet 4.6 (hosted API)       & 1.45\,s  & $\approx 6$ \\
    Opus 4.8 (hosted API)         & 1.53\,s  & $\approx 6$ \\
    Qwen2.5-VL 7B (local)         & 7.9\,s   & $\approx 31$ \\
    llama3.2-vision 11B (local)   & 12.1\,s  & $\approx 47$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{None clear the 270\,ms budget.} Even the \emph{fastest hosted frontier
model} is $\sim$4$\times$ too slow, and local open VLMs are 30--47$\times$. This
is the concrete version of ``VLMs are too slow to play games'': the whole
measured field would need the world \textbf{paused, or slowed 4--47$\times$}, to
act inside the window --- and the gap is a property of the \emph{time regime},
not the model, since $r$ here is $L/\text{window}$ with judgment held out
entirely. These are time-to-answer measurements, not judgments: in this fast
configuration the hosted models answered WAIT on nearly every frame (Haiku and
Sonnet 20/20, Opus 18/20) and the local models answered JUMP on nearly every
frame, so the numbers are each model's best case --- no extended thinking, and
a configuration that actually discriminates (the S5 judge, $\sim$5\,s/call) is
only slower. Each local model's first call is an order of magnitude faster than
the rest (0.4--0.6\,s against 8--12\,s), consistent with a warm cache; the
medians are unaffected. Local latency is on-device --- a real, reproducible
closed-loop number.

A closed-loop episode (blocking mode) confirms the local model is non-viable,
though the two failure modes should be separated: \code{llama3.2-vision} also
\emph{fails perception} --- it answered JUMP on all 20 frames (no depth
discrimination), so in blocking mode it jumps at tick 0 from $x \approx 2.6$
(far from the pit at $x = 10$) and falls. The latency result is the
axis-independent point: $r > \rstar$ dooms even a model that judges correctly,
the moment the world is allowed to run in real time.

\subsection{Control: action-repeat does not rescue (S3)}

Consulting the policy only every $k$ ticks and holding its last decision is a
common cost lever. It adds up to $k{-}1$ ticks of polling delay on top of the
explicit $D$, so it must shift the transition to \emph{lower} explicit $r$, and
it does. Measured $\rstar$ vs.\ $k$ at pit width 2 ($D \in \{0, 2, \dots, 40\}$,
one seed): $k{=}1 \to 1.10$, $k{=}2,4 \to 0.97$, $k{=}8,16 \to 0.71$; the ties
are grid quantization (a 2-tick delay step is $0.13$ in $r$ at this width). This
is a control, confirming that the apparatus charges polling delay the same as
injected delay. Action-repeat is a compute/cost lever, not a latency fix.

\section{Discussion}

The two failure axes have opposite fixes, and the framing makes the requirement
quantitative. A perception failure wants a better model: no amount of extra
time helps a judge that reports no distinguishable pit band under fog. A timing
failure wants a slower clock: a model that commits at the window's leading edge
is viable exactly when $r = L / T_{\text{window}} < \rstar \approx 1$, so a
deployment can cross the boundary by pausing the world ($r \to 0$), slowing it
(raising $T_{\text{window}}$), or cutting latency --- and the measured field
needs 4--47$\times$ on the latter two. S5 is the direct demonstration: the same
model, same judgment, clears 32/32 below the boundary and 0/26 above it, with
nothing varied but $r$. The practical reading is that pausable or turn-based
deployment converts every slow-but-accurate model we measured from failing to
viable, with no change to the model; conversely, a real-time benchmark score is
a product of judgment \emph{and} time regime, and ``VLMs are too slow to play
games'' is partly a fact about the clock they are handed. S5's delay-rescued
misjudgments add a sharper corollary: past the boundary, scores can even
\emph{reward} bad perception --- the axes compensate, and an aggregate
benchmark cannot tell.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The real-model sweep (S5) is one model at one pit width, at
    fog 0.} Its judge uses a revised prompt (the latency-battery prompt elicits
    no JUMPs near the window from any hosted model) plus a two-consecutive-JUMP
    commit rule, developed against and validated on the same 108 approach
    frames the sweep draws from --- a judge tuned to this scene, and a
    documented apparatus change, not the shipped default. Its $r$ axis is
    engineered by injected delay with the model's judgment in the loop; the
    model's own wall-clock latency ($\sim$5\,s/call at high effort) remains far
    past $\rstar$, so a wall-clock-real sweep is still impossible for the
    measured field --- that is the latency section's point, not a gap. The
    anticipated \emph{noisy} psychometric curve did not materialize --- this
    judge's decision variance is sub-tick --- so mapping a genuinely noisy
    transition needs a weaker or more stochastic judge.
  \item \textbf{The location $r \approx 1$ is definitional for leading-edge
    policies.} The agreement between the pit and aiming oracles (S1, S4) checks
    the apparatus; it is not evidence that real agents on other tasks share a
    boundary. Whether real judges on richer, multi-decision tasks have a stable
    commit point --- the quantity that would make $r$ predictive for them --- is
    untested; S5 shows one judge on one scene does.
  \item \textbf{The perception axis} mixes three Claude judges (run via a
    subagent path, not a controlled API benchmark) with one real keyless local
    VLM (\code{qwen2.5-vl:7b}), at $n = 10$ frames per condition --- large
    enough for the cross-model pattern, too small for per-model significance
    (Fisher exact $p = 0.14$--$0.35$). The open VLMs are weak/biased judges ---
    Qwen is JUMP-biased (60\% clear, no fog effect) and
    \code{llama3.2-vision:11b} is always-JUMP (contributes latency only, not a
    perception point). A controlled, latency-timed multi-provider API sweep
    still needs a key.
  \item \textbf{The latency battery measures time-to-answer, not judgment.} In
    the fast configuration the hosted models answered WAIT on nearly every
    frame; a configuration that discriminates is slower, so the measured $r$
    values are lower bounds. Latencies are network- and hardware-dependent, but
    they are real decision latencies, not stand-ins.
  \item \textbf{No human baseline} --- the oracle stands in as the
    perfect-judgment ceiling. Human psychophysics is future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}

Extending the S5 real-model sweep to more models, pit widths, and fog levels
(and to a judge noisy enough to trace a genuinely smooth psychometric curve); a
multi-provider latency panel (Gemini/GPT + more open VLMs); a human
psychophysics arm; and multi-decision tasks, to test whether real judges have a
stable enough commit point for $r$ to stay predictive beyond single reflexes.

\section*{Reproducibility}

Code, data, and figure-generating scripts are at
\url{https://github.com/star7js/judge-vs-react}, under \code{eval/pit/}
(analysis and sweeps) and \code{raycaster/} (the deterministic sim, including
\code{--task aim} for the second task). The perception judgments
(\code{perception\_judgments.jsonl}, 80 rows across 4 models), per-decision
latencies (\code{vlm\_latency.jsonl}, 100 rows across 5 models), the oracle
sweeps (default grid and the 1-tick \code{oracle\_edge\_sweep.jsonl}), the aim

latency numbers and the S5 sweep required one.
