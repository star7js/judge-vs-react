\title{\textbf{Judge vs.\ React:\\ dissociating perception from timing in VLM reflex control}}
\author{Josh Simnitt\\ \small\texttt{jsimnitt@gmail.com}}
\date{2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A vision-language-model (VLM) agent that must time one action in a
first-person scene can fail two ways that aggregate game scores cannot pull
apart: misjudge the scene, or judge correctly and act too late. We show the
conflation concretely. A closed-loop Sonnet~4.6 agent, judging every frame of
a deterministic first-person pit-jump task itself, clears \textbf{41/72}
episodes in clear weather and \textbf{41/72} under fog, for opposite reasons:
36 correct in-window decisions plus 5 early misjudgements carried into the
window by the control delay, versus 19 plus 22. Fog spread the judge's commit
point from one tick to five and raised early commits from 9 to 35; the delay
rescued most of the damage. All 144 episodes obey one rule: the episode
clears iff the commit position plus the distance travelled during the delay
lands in the window. Same score, different agents.

The apparatus reduces an episode to one decision, labels it in- or
out-of-window from the sim's own dynamics, and injects a control delay $D$ so
that lateness varies independently of judgment. In the coordinate $r = vD/W$
a leading-edge policy clears iff $r < 1$ by construction; oracle sweeps
($\rstar = 1.04$ on the pit, $0.98$ on an aiming task) confirm that the
apparatus reproduces this and serve as calibration, not as a law. Swept
through that boundary, the same Sonnet agent clears \textbf{32/32} in-window
decisions below $r \approx 1$ and \textbf{0/26} above it ($\rstar = 1.05$)
with nothing varied but the delay. Its commit point is nearly deterministic
(63/72 within one tick), so the transition is a step, not a psychometric
curve, and because it is asked on every one of $\sim$90 approach frames, a
1--2\% per-frame false-JUMP rate fires almost surely somewhere unless a
commit rule suppresses it.

On a balanced 100-frame perception battery through the API, no model judges
the depth cue unprompted; every hosted model does once the prompt names it
(91--99\%), fog then costs the smallest most (Haiku 97\% to 68\%), and a
local 7B model answers constantly either way: perception of this scene is a
property of the prompt. Five real models' decision latencies all exceed the
task's 257\,ms window ($r \approx 4$--$47$), so none can act in time in real
time, yet the same decisions succeed paused. ``VLMs are too slow to play
games'' is partly an artifact of the time regime they are benchmarked in, and
an aggregate score cannot tell a good judge from a lucky one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When a vision-language model plays a real-time game and walks into a pit, the
post-mortem is ambiguous. Did it fail to \emph{see} that a jump was needed
(perception), or did it see correctly and \emph{act too late} (timing)?
Aggregate game benchmarks --- score, win rate, levels cleared --- fold these two
failures into one number, so ``the VLM is bad at this game'' can mean either,
and usually means some unknown mix. The two have opposite fixes: a perception
failure wants a better model; a timing failure wants a slower clock. Conflating
them is why ``VLMs are too slow to play games'' is repeated as though it were a
single fact.

Our move is isolation. We reduce an episode to \emph{one} correct decision --- a
first-person pit the player auto-walks toward, where the only choice is
\emph{when} to jump --- so that on any given run exactly one of the two failures
can occur, and we can say which. The apparatus is deterministic and headless,
and it injects a control delay $D$ (a decision made at tick $t$ is applied at
$t{+}D$), which lets us vary \emph{lateness} completely independently of
\emph{judgment quality}: a perfect-judgment oracle still fails if its action
lands too late.

The headline result (\S\ref{sec:real}) is a closed-loop Sonnet~4.6 agent,
judging every frame itself, swept through the boundary by injected delay: it
clears 32/32 in-window decisions below $r \approx 1$ and 0/26 above it, and its
only clears past the boundary are \emph{delay-rescued misjudgments}. Around
it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Calibration (\S\ref{sec:calib}--\ref{sec:aim}).} For a policy
    that fires at the window's leading edge, clearing iff $r < 1$ is
    arithmetic, not a discovery. Oracle sweeps on the pit ($\rstar = 1.04$ on
    a 1-tick delay grid that brackets the edge at every width) and on a
    rotational aiming task ($\rstar = 0.98$) check that the sim's dynamics,
    the in-window label, and $r$ reproduce it without leaking tolerance, and
    the oracle's same-decision flip previews the real-model result on a
    perfect judge.
  \item \textbf{Two things the real judge revealed
    (\S\ref{sec:firstpassage}, \S\ref{sec:commit}).} A per-frame judge is a
    first-passage process: a false-JUMP rate that rounds to zero per frame
    fires almost surely per approach unless a commit rule suppresses it. And
    the judge's commit point is nearly deterministic, so its transition is a
    step rather than a curve.
  \item \textbf{Fog in closed loop (\S\ref{sec:fog}).} The same judge swept
    through the boundary under fog: its commit point spreads over five ticks,
    35/72 episodes commit early, the in-window boundary does not move, and 22
    of the early commits are rescued by delay. Both sweeps score 41/72.
  \item \textbf{Perception axis (\S\ref{sec:perception}).} Through the API,
    no model judges the depth cue unprompted; every model does once the
    prompt names it; fog then costs the smallest model most. An earlier
    subagent-judged pilot claiming unprompted depth reading is retracted.
  \item \textbf{Latency axis (\S\ref{sec:latency}).} Five real vision models'
    decision latencies placed on the same axis: at this sim speed all are
    past the boundary, hosted frontier models at $r \approx 4$--$6$ and local
    open VLMs at $r \approx 31$--$47$.
  \item \textbf{Control (Appendix~\ref{app:repeat}).} Action-repeat adds
    polling delay and shifts the transition to lower explicit $r$; it is a
    cost lever, not a latency fix.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

Five works are closest; we differentiate from each.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OmniGameArena} (Lin et al., 2026, arXiv:2606.09826) --- has
    paused (PDQ) vs.\ latency-controlled real-time (LCRT) clock modes, but only
    as \textbf{aggregate whole-game} comparisons; no per-decision in-window
    label, no single-decision flip. We add both, and the $r$ coordinate that
    makes latency measurements comparable across tasks.
  \item \textbf{VideoGameBench} (Zhang et al., 2025, arXiv:2505.18134) ---
    ``Lite'' (paused) vs.\ real-time; names the stale-action mechanism but
    leaves perception confounded (``it's not just latency''). We resolve exactly
    that confound by isolating one decision.
  \item \textbf{``Win Fast or Lose Slow''} (Kang et al., NeurIPS 2025,
    arXiv:2505.19481) --- owns the latency--quality tradeoff, but its latency
    lever is quantization/model-size, which \emph{also} degrades quality, and
    its $\approx$200\,ms point is an environment action-rate cap, not a
    task-window threshold. Our sweep varies latency \textbf{independently} of
    the model --- the apparatus injects control delay $D$ with the policy
    fixed.
  \item \textbf{BLINK} (Fu et al., ECCV 2024, arXiv:2404.12390) --- canonical
    ``MLLMs fail at depth,'' but static MCQ. Ours is interactive, parametric,
    oracle-normalized.
  \item \textbf{Ramstedt \& Pal, ``Real-Time RL''} (NeurIPS 2019,
    arXiv:1911.04448) [+ ``At Human Speed,'' arXiv:1810.07286] --- the RL parent
    of the real-time/delay result (monotone degradation for learned policies).
    We express the same delay cost as $r$ for \emph{pausable VLM agents}, show
    a real VLM crossing it, and show turn-based deployment stepping back over
    it.
\end{itemize}

We explicitly do \textbf{not} claim to be first to study the latency--quality
tradeoff (Win Fast) nor first to isolate timing from competence in the aggregate
(Real-Time Deadlines, arXiv:2601.13206; OmniGameArena). Our claim is the
single-decision dissociation on a real model, the compensating-axes observation
past the boundary, and the quality-controlled $r$ coordinate that makes the
latency measurements interpretable.

\section{Task and apparatus}

A first-person raycaster. The player auto-walks toward a pit; the only decision
is \emph{when} to jump. Knobs: pit width (sets the action-window $W$),
fog/visibility (perception), approach speed $v$, seed. The sim is deterministic
and headless; control delay $D$ (ticks) is \textbf{injected} --- a decision made
at tick $t$ is applied at $t{+}D$ --- so $D$ is an independent variable
decoupled from wall-clock. Real per-model latency $L$ is measured separately
(timed calls) and mapped to the $r$ axis via $L/\text{tick}$. An oracle policy
(true pit distance) is the perfect-judgment ceiling.

\paragraph{Ground-truth label.} \code{in\_window} $=$ whether a delay-0 takeoff
at the decision position clears, computed from the sim's \emph{own} dynamics
(not an analytic approximation), so \code{in\_window} exactly predicts
\code{cleared} at $D{=}0$. The action window is $W = w_{hi} - w_{lo}$ with
$w_{lo}, w_{hi}$ derived by simulating takeoffs; for pit widths beyond the jump
reach the window is empty ($W \le 0$, unclearable) and those configs are
excluded from fits (reported, not silently dropped).
\[
  r = \frac{v \cdot D}{W}\ \text{(oracle sweep)}
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  r = \frac{v \cdot (L/\text{tick})}{W}\ \text{(real-model placement).}
\]

\paragraph{What $r$ does and does not tell you.} A policy that commits at the
window's leading edge $w_{lo}$ has its delayed takeoff land at $w_{lo} + vD$,
which clears iff $vD < W$, i.e.\ iff $r < 1$. The location of the boundary is
therefore fixed by where the policy fires, not discovered by sweeping; a policy
that fired at the window's center would place it at $r = 0.5$. $r$ is the
coordinate in which that statement is exact and task-independent, which is what
makes a measured latency $L$ interpretable against any window. The oracle
sweeps in \S\ref{sec:calib}--\ref{sec:aim} are calibration: they check that the dynamics, the label,
and $r$ reproduce the boundary with no leaked tolerance. The question with
empirical content --- where a \emph{real} judge's commit point falls, and
whether its outcome flips at that boundary with nothing else varied --- is
\S\ref{sec:real}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Calibration: the oracle boundary}\label{sec:calib}

The oracle fires at the first in-window position, so a delayed takeoff lands at
$w_{lo} + vD$ and clears iff $r < 1$; the sweep's job is to confirm that the
apparatus reproduces this. It does. The default grid (4 pit widths $\times$ 7
delays, $D \in \{0, 4, \dots, 24\}$) has 28 unique configurations. The 1680
committed rows replicate each $60\times$ across fog and seed, which the oracle
ignores (every replicate is identical), and the 7 configurations at pit width 4
have an empty window ($W \le 0$, unclearable) and are excluded. On the
remaining 21 configurations the pooled logistic fit gives $\rstar = 1.17$, but
that number is a grid artifact: the last clear is at $r = 1.04$ and the first
failure at $r = 1.30$, both at pit width 2, and $1.17$ is the logistic midpoint
of a 4-tick gap. The other widths do not straddle the edge within this grid ---
width 1 ($W = 2.08$) never exceeds $r = 0.81$ and always clears, and width 3
($W = 0.08$) is already at $r = 3.5$ at the first nonzero delay and always
fails.

A 1-tick delay grid ($D \in \{0, \dots, 40\}$, widths 1--3, one seed, fog 0;
123 configurations) removes the artifact. Each width brackets the edge within a
single tick of delay: width 1 between $r = 1.01$ ($D = 30$) and $1.04$
($D = 31$), width 2 between $1.04$ ($D = 16$) and $1.10$ ($D = 17$), and width 3
between $0.89$ ($D = 1$) and $1.77$ ($D = 2$), its window being only 1.1 ticks
wide. Every width is a clean step (every delay below its edge clears, every
delay at or above it fails), and the pooled logistic fit gives
$\rstar = 1.04$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:edge}). This is the calibration number used
below. The clears just past $r = 1$ are physical: a jump that fires on the same
tick can take off from just past the near edge, extending tolerance a fraction
of a tick beyond the nominal window. The transition is a step because the
oracle is deterministic.

\subsection{Same-decision flip on the oracle}\label{sec:flip}

Restricting to oracle-correct decisions (\code{in\_window} $= 1$; the oracle's
commit position does not depend on $D$), the \textbf{identical} decision clears
3/3 unique configurations at $D = 0$ and 0/8 once $r \ge 1.30$. The one
in-window decision past $r = 1$ that clears is the $r = 1.04$ same-tick case
above. Only execution timing changed. We do not summarize this as a pooled
probability: six of the 9 configurations with $r > 1$ are at pit width 3, where
$r$ runs from $3.5$ to $21$, so a rate over them would describe the delay grid
rather than the task. The flip on a real judge, with decisions on both sides of
the edge, is \S\ref{sec:real}.

\subsection{The same calibration on a second task}\label{sec:aim}

Is the apparatus's behaviour specific to jump-timing? We built a second task in
a different modality: a \textbf{sweeping-aim} task. The player rotates in place
at constant angular speed $\omega$; a target subtends an angular window
$[\theta - h, \theta + h]$ (width $2h$); the single decision is \emph{fire}, and
a control delay $D$ rotates the aim $\omega D$ further before the shot lands ---
overshoot if it exceeds the window. By construction the dimensionless cost is
$r = \omega D / (2h)$, the exact angular analog of the pit's $vD/W$. The aim
episode is pure kinematics and emits the same schema, so the \emph{identical}
fit machinery applies.

Because the aim oracle also fires at the leading edge, a hit iff $r < 1$ is
again arithmetic; the test is whether an independent implementation of the
kinematics and the label reproduces it. It does: across 205 unique
configurations at five target widths (4.6\textdegree--13.8\textdegree; one
seed, the aim oracle being deterministic) the pooled logistic fit gives
$\rstar = 0.98$, and at every width the last hit lies at $r = 0.87$--$0.96$ and
the first miss at exactly $r = 1.00$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:aim}). The offset from the
pit's $1.04$ is the oracles' firing phase: the pit's takeoff can extend just
\emph{past} the near edge (a hair above 1), while the aim fires just
\emph{inside} the near edge, forfeiting a sub-tick of tolerance (a hair below
1). Two tasks agreeing on $r \approx 1$ shows that $r$ is the right coordinate
for both and that neither implementation leaks tolerance. It is not evidence of
an empirical law: a policy that fired at the window's center would place its
boundary at $r = 0.5$ in the same apparatus. What the next section asks is
where a real judge's commit point actually falls.

\subsection{A real model through the boundary}\label{sec:real}

The oracle sections calibrate the boundary; here a real model is swept
\emph{through} it with its own judgment in the loop. Because the apparatus
decouples sim time from wall-clock, the model's (slow, variable) API latency is
free, and $r$ is engineered exactly as in \S\ref{sec:calib}: Sonnet~4.6 judges
\emph{every grounded frame} of a closed-loop blocking episode (pit width 2,
$v = 0.07$, fog 0, seeds 1--6), its committed jump is applied $D$ ticks later,
and sweeping $D \in \{0, \dots, 31\}$ sweeps $r = vD/W$ from $0$ to $2$,
densely around $r = 1$ (72 episodes; 6{,}612 vision calls).

\paragraph{Judge protocol.} The episode prompt used for the latency battery
(\S\ref{sec:latency}) turns out never to elicit JUMP near the window from
\emph{any} hosted model: it describes the pit as ``the black band across the
floor ahead,'' which stops matching the close-range view --- inside the window
the pit is a black region filling the bottom of the frame, and the visible
checkered floor is the pit's \emph{far} side. The judge used here instead
answers a literal perceptual question (``what touches the bottom border of the
image: tiles or black?'') at high reasoning effort, and commits only on two
consecutive JUMP answers; \S\ref{sec:firstpassage} explains why that commit
rule is forced rather than cosmetic. We validated the final judge on all 108
approach frames $\times$ 3 samples (Fig.~\ref{fig:firstpassage}, left): zero
JUMPs on the first 79 frames, isolated JUMPs at $x = 8.10$ (1/3) and $8.24$
(2/3), and unanimous JUMP from $x = 8.87$ on. This judge was developed against,
and validated on, the same renderer, pit width, and fog level the sweep uses;
it is a judge tuned to this scene, and we make no claim that it transfers. The
bottom-edge cue is nearly ideal in this renderer: the pit's black region first
touches the frame's bottom edge at $x = 9.01$, one tick after the window opens
($w_{lo} = 8.92$).

\paragraph{The boundary holds for a real judge.} Episodes whose decision lands
in the window clear \textbf{32/32 through $r = 0.97$}, 4/5 at $r = 1.04$, and
\textbf{0/26 from $r = 1.10$ on}; the same logistic fit as \S\ref{sec:calib}
gives $\rstar = 1.05$, coinciding with the oracle's width-2 bracket
$[1.04, 1.10]$ on the 1-tick grid: the real judge's edge sits where the
perfect judge's does, same-tick tolerance included (Fig.~\ref{fig:realmodel}).
The transition is nearly as sharp as the oracle's because the model's commit
position is almost deterministic (\S\ref{sec:commit}); given that commit point,
the \emph{location} of $\rstar$ follows from the kinematics exactly as in
\S\ref{sec:calib}. What this sweep establishes that the oracle cannot is the
dissociation itself on a real judge: the same model, making the same
frame-by-frame decisions, clears every in-window decision below the boundary
and none above it, with nothing varied but $D$.

\paragraph{Delay-rescued misjudgments.} 9/72 episodes commit early
($x = 8.10$--$8.24$, out of window --- a perception error; these are excluded
from the timing fit above). At $D \le 8$ every such episode fails, landing
short. In the band $D = 15$--$24$ ($r = 0.97$--$1.56$), \emph{every} early episode
\textbf{clears} (5/5): the delay pushes the too-early takeoff into the window.
Past $r \approx 1.1$ the \emph{only} surviving episodes are the misjudged
ones; by $r = 2.01$ the delay overshoots even the early takeoff and the
rescue band closes. The two failure axes do not merely
dissociate --- on opposite sides of the boundary they \emph{compensate}, which
is only visible because the apparatus labels each decision in- or
out-of-window.

\subsection{A frame-by-frame judge is a first-passage process}\label{sec:firstpassage}

The commit rule above is not a prompt-engineering detail; the structure of the
task forces it. A frame-by-frame controller is consulted on every approach
frame, and the episode is decided by the \emph{first} JUMP it commits to, so
any per-frame false-positive rate compounds over the approach. With a
per-frame false-JUMP rate $p$ over the $N \approx 90$ frames before the window,
a single-JUMP commit rule fires somewhere early with probability
$1 - (1-p)^N$: \textbf{60\% at $p = 1\%$ and 84\% at $p = 2\%$}, the rates we
measured for earlier prompt versions far from the pit. Under the same
independent-error assumption the two-consecutive rule fires with probability
0.9\% and 3.4\% respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:firstpassage}, right; exact
two-state chain). A single-frame accuracy battery --- the standard way to
evaluate a VLM judge --- scores a 1\% false-positive rate as 99\% accuracy and
cannot see this. The final judge's own battery (Fig.~\ref{fig:firstpassage},
left) shows the residual: its only pre-window JUMPs are in the tile before the
window ($x = 8.10$--$8.24$), and committing there requires two consecutive
misfires, which is what the sweep's 9/72 early episodes are. Its unanimous JUMP
from $x = 8.87$, one frame before the window opens, is converted by the commit
rule into a commit at $8.94$, inside the window. The residual errors are not
independent across frames --- they cluster where the floor sliver below the pit
becomes thin --- so the independent-error curves are an idealization; the
mechanism is not. Any deployment of a VLM as a per-frame trigger needs a commit
rule or an equivalent, and needs to be evaluated per episode, not per frame.

\subsection{The commit point is nearly deterministic}\label{sec:commit}

The sweep's in-window decisions commit at $x = 8.94$ in 51/72 episodes and at
$x = 9.01$ in 12 --- within one tick of each other across 63 episodes and six
seeds, with the judge's own sampling stochasticity (adaptive thinking at high
effort) in the loop. We expected a noisy psychometric curve and got a
near-step. Two readings. The optimistic one: on a rendered depth cue with a
pixel-sharp onset, a frontier VLM's judgment is effectively deterministic at
the tick scale, so in a deployment like this one timing failures are failures
of latency, not of judgment variance --- the clean separation this paper's
framing assumes. The cautious one: the judge was tuned to this cue, and its
one-tick lead (answering ``black'' at $8.94$, before the region literally
reaches the bottom edge at $9.01$) shows it is reading a thinning sliver of
floor rather than the edge itself. A cue without a sharp onset would presumably
spread the commit point, and that spread is the quantity a real-time deployment
needs to know, because it sets how much of the window is left after the commit.
Measuring it on weaker cues (fog) and weaker judges is the obvious next
experiment.

\subsection{The same judge through the boundary under fog}\label{sec:fog}

The single-frame battery of \S\ref{sec:perception} says fog costs the
cue-prompted Sonnet nothing per frame. The \S\ref{sec:real} judge's own
battery under fog (108 approach frames $\times$ 3 samples at high effort)
says it costs something per approach: the
judge still fires on every in-window frame, but it also fires early more
often --- isolated JUMPs at $x = 8.38$, $8.73$ and $8.80$, a far-field false
JUMP at $x = 2.85$ (1/3), and two in-window frames that were not unanimous.
We therefore repeated the \S\ref{sec:real} sweep at fog 1: same judge, same
prompt, same 12 delays $\times$ 6 seeds (72 episodes; 6{,}669 vision calls),
with nothing changed but the renderer's fog (Fig.~\ref{fig:fog}).

\paragraph{The commit point spreads.} In clear weather 63/72 commits sat at
$x = 8.94$ or $9.01$, inside the window. Under fog the commits spread over
five ticks --- $8.80$ (11), $8.87$ (24), $8.94$ (19), $9.01$ (17), $9.08$ (1)
--- and \textbf{35/72 land before the window opens}, against 9/72 in clear
weather. This is the perception failure in closed loop: the same prompt reads
the thinning sliver of floor less reliably when fog lowers its contrast, and
the judge fires a frame or two early about half the time. It is also the
commit-point spread that \S\ref{sec:commit} asked for, obtained by weakening
the cue rather than the judge.

\paragraph{The timing boundary does not move.} Among in-window commits the
law is as sharp as before: \textbf{19/19 clear through $r = 0.97$ and 0/18
from $r = 1.04$ on}; the logistic fit gives $\rstar = 1.01$ (clear weather:
$1.05$). The small shift is the commit-offset term of the Discussion: the fog
judge's in-window commits at $D = 16$ sat at $9.01$ and $9.08$ rather than
$8.94$, so less of the window was left and the same delay overshot. Across
both sweeps every one of the 144 episodes obeys a single rule --- it clears
iff the takeoff lands inside the window extended by the same-tick tolerance,
$w_{lo} \le x_{\text{commit}} + vD \le 10.06$ --- so the judge's entire
contribution to the outcome is where it commits.

\paragraph{The axes compensate at scale.} 22 of the 35 early commits clear:
21 with $4 \le D \le 17$, where the delay carries an $8.80$ or $8.87$ takeoff
into the window, and one $8.80$ commit at $D = 18$ that lands exactly on the
tolerance edge. Below the boundary the perception errors are
invisible (21/21 early commits in that band clear, exactly like the correct
decisions), and at $D = 17$ ($r = 1.10$) the only four clears in six episodes
are misjudgments. \textbf{Both sweeps clear exactly 41/72.} In clear weather
that is 36 correct decisions plus 5 rescued errors; under fog it is 19 plus
22. An aggregate success rate cannot tell these apart. Pooling all 72 fog
episodes into one logistic fit even produces the smooth psychometric curve a
benchmark would report ($\rstar = 1.07$, slope 14, against slope 409 for the
in-window fit) --- a curve that describes the mixture of correct and early
commits, not the judge.

\subsection{Perception axis}\label{sec:perception}

Does a vision model judge this jump from a single frame? We measured it
through the API, blind, on a balanced battery. For each fog level we render
100 standing frames at pit width 2 --- 50 at distinct out-of-window positions
($x = 6.00$--$8.85$) and 50 inside the window ($x = 8.93$--$9.99$) --- shuffle
them into neutral names with a fixed seed, and show each alone (no
coordinates, window, or labels) to three hosted models and one local open
model, which answer JUMP or WAIT; each answer is scored against the sim's \code{in\_window} label, so
chance on this set is 50\%. We ran two prompts. The \emph{neutral} prompt
describes the task (auto-walking toward a pit you must jump, too early lands
short, too late walks in) and says nothing about what the pit looks like or
where in the frame to look. The \emph{cue} prompt is the \S\ref{sec:real}
judge's: it tells the model to read the strip touching the bottom border of
the frame and answer tiles or black. Sonnet~4.6 and Opus~4.8 run adaptive
thinking at medium effort; Haiku~4.5 predates adaptive thinking and runs with
a 2k-token thinking budget; Qwen2.5-VL~7B runs locally through Ollama with
no thinking. 1{,}600 calls in all (Table~\ref{tab:perception},
Fig.~\ref{fig:perception}).

Four things fall out. \textbf{(1) Unprompted, no model judges the cue at
all.} With the neutral prompt every model is at chance at both fog levels;
Sonnet and Opus answered WAIT on 199 of 200 frames each without engaging
extended thinking, Qwen on all 200, and Haiku's JUMPs are scattered across
the approach.
\textbf{(2) Told where to look, every model judges it, including the
smallest.} The cue prompt lifts clear-weather accuracy to 97\% (Haiku), 91\%
(Sonnet) and 99\% (Opus); each contrast with the neutral prompt is Fisher
exact $p < 10^{-9}$. \textbf{(3) Fog degrades the cued judgment where the
model is weakest, not where it is strongest.} Haiku falls from 97\% to 68\%
($p = 3.5 \times 10^{-8}$), missing 30 of 50 in-window frames; Opus from 99\%
to 94\% ($p = 0.12$); Sonnet is unchanged (91\% vs.\ 93\%, $p = 0.8$), its
clear-weather errors being false JUMPs in the tile before the window
($x = 7.98$--$8.73$, 9/50 at medium effort) rather than misses. The capability
ordering is visible only under fog and only with the cue. \textbf{(4) The
local 7B model never judges it.} Qwen2.5-VL answered WAIT on every neutral
frame and JUMP on every cue frame, clear or fog; under the cue prompt it
echoed the prompt's first line verbatim instead of answering it, then emitted
JUMP. Both arms are a constant answer, so both sit at exactly 50\% on the
balanced set. A model that follows the cue instruction (the hosted three)
and one that cannot (Qwen) are indistinguishable with the neutral prompt and
49 points apart with the cue.

\paragraph{Retraction of the pilot result.} An earlier draft reported, from a
10-frame-per-cell battery judged by Claude Code subagents rather than API
calls, that Opus and Sonnet read the depth cue unprompted (90\% and 80\%) and
collapsed to chance under fog, and called the collapse a signature of genuine
depth reading. That result does not replicate under the controlled protocol
above: unprompted, the same models are at chance in both conditions. We keep
the pilot file in the repository for the record
(\code{perception\_judgments.jsonl}) and withdraw the claim; the subagent path
evidently supplied context that a single-frame API call does not.

This reframes the perception half of the dissociation. ``Perception'' of this
scene is not a fixed property of the model: it is almost entirely whether the
prompt names the cue, and once it does, capability shows up as the robustness
of that reading to fog. Both measurements are at zero delay and independent
of the timing axis. The \S\ref{sec:real} judge is the cue-prompted Sonnet at
high effort, so its per-frame accuracy at fog 0 is the 91\% cell above with
the effort raised; \S\ref{sec:firstpassage} shows why a 9/50 pre-window
false-JUMP rate at medium effort would be fatal in closed loop, and
\S\ref{sec:fog} is that judge taken through the boundary under fog.

\subsection{Placing real models' latency on the axis}\label{sec:latency}

$r = L / T_{\text{window}}$ places a measured decision latency on the same
axis. At pit width 2 and $v = 0.07$ tiles/tick the window is open for
$T_{\text{window}} = (W/v)\cdot\text{tick} = 257\,$ms (tick $= 1/60\,$s), so the
calibrated boundary corresponds to $\rstar\cdot T_{\text{window}} \approx
270\,$ms. We timed five vision models on the same 20-frame battery, in each
model's fastest single-call configuration (Table~\ref{tab:latency},
Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}): hosted frontier models answer in 1--1.5\,s
($r \approx 4$--$6$) and local open VLMs in 8--12\,s ($r \approx 31$--$47$).
None is inside the window at this speed. The multiples are a property of the
speed we chose --- $T_{\text{window}}$ scales as $1/v$, and a world slowed
$4\times$ puts the hosted models at the boundary --- which is the point: $r$
is the quantity to report, and a deployment moves it by pausing or slowing the
world as readily as by cutting latency. Two caveats on the measurement. These
are time-to-answer numbers, not judgments: in the fast configuration the hosted
models answered WAIT on nearly every frame (Haiku and Sonnet 20/20, Opus
18/20; the unprompted default of \S\ref{sec:perception}) and the local models
JUMP on nearly every frame; a configuration that
discriminates (the \S\ref{sec:real} judge, $\sim$5\,s/call) is slower, so these
$r$ values are lower bounds. And each local model's first call is an order of
magnitude faster than the rest (0.4--0.6\,s against 8--12\,s), consistent with
a warm cache; the medians are unaffected.

\section{Discussion}

The two failure axes have opposite fixes, and the framing makes the requirement
quantitative. A perception failure wants a better model: no amount of extra
time helps a judge that reports no distinguishable pit band under fog. A timing
failure wants a slower clock: a judge that commits a fraction $\delta$ of the
window past its leading edge is viable exactly when
$r = L / T_{\text{window}} < 1 - \delta$ (plus the same-tick tolerance). For
the oracle and for the Sonnet judge of \S\ref{sec:real}, $\delta \approx 0.02$,
which is why both sit at $\rstar \approx 1$; a judge that committed mid-window
would have $\rstar = 0.5$ in the same apparatus, and the spread of its commit
point (\S\ref{sec:commit}) would turn the step into a curve. A deployment can
cross the boundary by pausing the world ($r \to 0$), slowing it (raising
$T_{\text{window}}$), or cutting latency --- and at this sim speed the measured
field needs 4--47$\times$ on the latter two. \S\ref{sec:real} is the direct
demonstration: the same model, same judgment, clears 32/32 below the boundary
and 0/26 above it, with nothing varied but $r$. The practical reading is that
pausable or turn-based deployment converts every slow-but-accurate model we
measured from failing to viable, with no change to the model; conversely, a
real-time benchmark score is a product of judgment \emph{and} time regime, and
``VLMs are too slow to play games'' is partly a fact about the clock they are
handed. The delay-rescued misjudgments add a sharper corollary: past the
boundary, scores can even \emph{reward} bad perception --- the axes
compensate, and an aggregate benchmark cannot tell. The fog sweep
(\S\ref{sec:fog}) makes the compensation quantitative: the same aggregate
score, 41/72, is 36 correct plus 5 rescued in clear weather and 19 plus 22
under fog, and pooling the fog episodes yields a smooth psychometric curve
that belongs to the mixture, not to the judge. And the first-passage result
(\S\ref{sec:firstpassage}) says that even below the boundary, a per-frame
judge has to be evaluated per episode: a false-positive rate that rounds to
zero per frame does not round to zero per approach.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The real-model sweeps (\S\ref{sec:real}, \S\ref{sec:fog})
    are one model at one pit width, at two fog levels.} Its judge uses a revised prompt (the latency-battery
    prompt elicits no JUMPs near the window from any hosted model) plus a
    two-consecutive-JUMP commit rule, developed against and validated on the
    same 108 approach frames the sweep draws from --- a judge tuned to this
    scene, and a documented apparatus change, not the shipped default. Its $r$
    axis is engineered by injected delay with the model's judgment in the
    loop; the model's own wall-clock latency ($\sim$5\,s/call at high effort)
    remains far past $\rstar$, so a wall-clock-real sweep is still impossible
    for the measured field --- that is \S\ref{sec:latency}'s point, not a gap.
    The anticipated \emph{noisy} psychometric curve did not materialize in
    either sweep: fog spread the commit point, but mostly \emph{before} the
    window, and outcome is a deterministic function of commit position and
    delay, so the in-window transition stayed a step. A smooth in-window
    curve needs a judge whose commit varies within the window.
  \item \textbf{The location $r \approx 1$ is definitional for leading-edge
    policies.} The agreement between the pit and aiming oracles
    (\S\ref{sec:calib}, \S\ref{sec:aim}) checks the apparatus; it is not
    evidence that real agents on other tasks share a boundary. Whether real
    judges on richer, multi-decision tasks have a stable commit point --- the
    quantity that would make $r$ predictive for them --- is untested;
    \S\ref{sec:commit} shows one judge on one scene does.
  \item \textbf{The first-passage curves assume independent per-frame
    errors.} The final judge's residual errors cluster in the tile before the
    window, so the curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:firstpassage} are an idealization of
    the mechanism, and the 1--2\% per-frame rates they are evaluated at come
    from earlier prompt versions, not from the final judge (whose far-field
    rate on the battery was zero in 237 samples).
  \item \textbf{The perception axis is one scene, two prompts, three hosted
    models at medium effort and one local 7B model.} The cue prompt is the \S\ref{sec:real} judge's,
    so the cue-arm numbers are that judge's single-frame accuracy at a lower
    effort than the sweep used; a prompt that named a different cue, or a
    scene without a pixel-sharp cue, could land anywhere between the two
    arms. Judges are blind to coordinates and labels but not to the task. The
    local model's chance result is an instruction-following failure (a
    constant answer), not a measurement of its depth perception.
  \item \textbf{The latency battery measures time-to-answer, not judgment,
    at one arbitrary sim speed.} In the fast configuration the hosted models
    answered WAIT on nearly every frame; a configuration that discriminates is
    slower, so the measured $r$ values are lower bounds. The ``$4$--$47\times$''
    multiples scale with $1/v$ and would halve at half the approach speed; $r$
    is the portable number. Latencies are network- and hardware-dependent, but
    they are real decision latencies, not stand-ins.
  \item \textbf{No human baseline} --- the oracle stands in as the
    perfect-judgment ceiling. Human psychophysics is future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}

Extending the real-model sweeps to more models and pit widths; a cue without
a sharp onset, to spread the commit point inside the window rather than
before it (the quantity that would turn the step into a psychometric curve); a multi-provider latency
panel (Gemini/GPT + more open VLMs); a human psychophysics arm; and
multi-decision tasks, to test whether real judges have a stable enough commit
point for $r$ to stay predictive beyond single reflexes.

\section{Control: action-repeat does not rescue}\label{app:repeat}

Consulting the policy only every $k$ ticks and holding its last decision is a
common cost lever. It adds up to $k{-}1$ ticks of polling delay on top of the
explicit $D$, so it must shift the transition to \emph{lower} explicit $r$, and
it does. Measured $\rstar$ vs.\ $k$ at pit width 2 ($D \in \{0, 2, \dots, 40\}$,
one seed): $k{=}1 \to 1.10$, $k{=}2,4 \to 0.97$, $k{=}8,16 \to 0.71$; the ties
are grid quantization (a 2-tick delay step is $0.13$ in $r$ at this width). This
is a control, confirming that the apparatus charges polling delay the same as
injected delay. Action-repeat is a compute/cost lever, not a latency fix.
